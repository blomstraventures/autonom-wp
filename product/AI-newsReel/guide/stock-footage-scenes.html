Stock Footage for News Shorts:
Matching Every Scene Without Misleading

A stock clip is not footage of the event. It is footage of some event, filmed somewhere, at some point, by someone with a camera. When a news Short pairs a sentence about a specific missile strike with a stock clip of a missile launching, the viewer assumes the clip is the strike. It is not. It is a stock clip, from a different context, doing a job that only a label can fix — and often not even then. That is the whole problem of stock footage in news, and most AI video tools ignore it.

This guide is about getting that right, scene by scene. What footage does and does not belong in a news video, how to search for it without falling into the misleading-clip trap, when a plain caption card beats any clip at all, and why an illustrative-footage label exists. It is the pair to the voice guide — that one is about the sound, this one is about the picture.

⚠
Generic footage of a missile is not footage of this missile.

A stock clip of a missile launch is footage of some missile launch, somewhere, at some time. If the video pairs it with a sentence about a specific strike, the viewer will assume the clip is the strike. That is a misleading video, even when every word in the script is true.

Three ways footage goes wrong in news videos

There are three shapes the footage problem takes, and they have different causes and different fixes. Most AI-generated news videos have at least one of them, and most creators do not notice because the video looks fine — it plays, the captions read, the audio is in sync.

1. Misleading footage

The clip shows one thing; the sentence is about another. A missile launch clip under a sentence about the missile that hit a hospital. A tank column under a sentence about a specific brigade. A protest crowd under a sentence about a specific march. The clip is plausible, the sentence is accurate, and the pairing invents a claim neither of them made on its own.

What actually happens: The video is technically factually correct and morally misleading at the same time. If someone watches it and later quotes the video, they will quote the impression, not the sentence. This is what the illustrative-footage label exists to prevent, and no label can prevent it when the footage was a bad choice to begin with.

2. Mismatched footage

The clip has nothing to do with the sentence, but it also does not imply anything specific. A stock clip of a stock exchange under a sentence about a specific currency's movement. A clip of a generic government building under a sentence about a specific ministry. The clip is not misleading in the sense of inventing a claim, but it is decorative — it does not illustrate anything the sentence is actually saying, so it does the video no work.

What actually happens: The viewer notices that the video is not really showing anything. After two or three mismatched scenes, the visual track stops carrying meaning, and the viewer's attention drops. Mismatched footage is not a lie; it is a wasted scene.

3. Dead air between clips

The clip is fine and the sentence is fine, but there is a gap: a scene where the search returned nothing, or the clip is too short to fill the sentence, or the tool gave up and dropped to a blank card without warning. The result is a scene with no visual and a voice that keeps talking. On a 45-second Short, one dead scene is enough to lose half the audience.

What actually happens: The video plays, but it stops feeling like a video. It feels like a podcast with a decorative slideshow, and the viewer's thumb knows the difference.

The three failures share one cause: the footage is being treated as a decoration for the sentence, rather than a decision about the sentence. A clip that is chosen because it "roughly matches" is a clip that fails in one of the three ways above, because "roughly matches" is not a specification. A good scene has a specific job, and the job decides the clip — or decides that there should be no clip at all.

How to match footage scene by scene

The good news is that matching is a learnable skill, and it produces footage that is both more accurate and more engaging than the "roughly matches" default. The bad news is that the skill is the opposite of what most people do, because most people search for stock footage the way they would search for a stock photo on a stock site — by topic, not by shot.

Here are the four rules that turn the search into a match. All of them can be applied by hand in Pexels or Pixabay, and all of them are what Autonom's scene planner does automatically.

1. Search in verb-noun, not in topic

The intuitive search is the topic: missile, sanctions, inflation, election. The result is generic — a missile somewhere, a sanctions-related abstraction, a chart with an arrow going up. The search that actually works is a verb plus a noun, describing the shot you need: missile launch, protest crowd, oil tanker, central bank building. A verb-noun search gives the stock library something to shoot against, and it gives the video a scene, not a symbol.

2. Search without names

Stock libraries do not have footage of specific named people, places, or organisations. They have footage of a government building, a port, a crowd. If you search for Kremlin, you will get a result — usually a generic Moscow skyline — and the viewer will read that as "this is the Kremlin", which it might not be. Search for government building or city skyline instead, and the footage is honest about being a generic shot. The viewer knows what they are looking at, and the illustrative label does the rest.

3. Prefer establishing shots to action shots

An establishing shot — a wide view of a city, a building, a coastline — is safe. It suggests a place without claiming a specific event. An action shot — a missile launch, an explosion, a crowd surging — is not safe, because it is indistinguishable from footage of the actual event. For a news Short, the establishing shot is almost always the better choice: it holds the viewer's eye without implying that the video has footage it does not have. Save the action shots for stories where the action itself is the point and no claim is being made about which specific action it was.

4. When the sentence is abstract, use a caption card

Not every sentence has a shot. "The decision is a turning point for the alliance." "Whether this holds depends on two things." "The question now is what Brussels does next." Those sentences are abstract, and there is no stock clip that illustrates them without implying a claim the sentence is not making. A plain caption card — big text on a dark background, no footage — is not a fallback; it is the right choice. It keeps the visual track clean and it does not pretend to illustrate something that is not visual.

The four rules are not contradictory; they are a decision tree. A concrete, specific sentence gets a verb-noun search. A sentence about a place gets an establishing shot. A sentence about an abstract idea gets a caption card. The scene planner's job is to make that decision per sentence, before any search happens, so the search has a spec to match instead of a topic to riff on.

How Autonom plans footage

Autonom Newsreel does not search first and decide later. It plans first, and the plan decides whether a scene gets footage or a card, and what to search for if it gets footage. That is a small architectural choice with a large effect on the result, and it is worth describing because it is what most AI video tools do not do.

Step 1 — the scene planner. The script is split into sentences. Each sentence is a scene. The planner reads the whole script with the story's title and the channel's voice, and for each scene decides two things: whether the scene is footage or card, and if footage, what to search for. A scene about a specific event, a place, a machine, a crowd, or an object is footage. A scene that is a claim, an opinion, a statistic, a question to the viewer, or the closing call-to-action is card. The planner does not try to find footage for abstract sentences; it explicitly chooses the card. The output is a per-scene plan, and it is shown to the user, not hidden.

Step 2 — the search query. For each footage scene, the planner writes a 2–4 word verb-noun query, plus an alternate query that is more generic and safe. Neither query contains a name. Neither query is a single abstract word. If a scene is about a port strike, the query is shipping port cranes, not strike. If a scene is about a government meeting, the query is government building exterior, not European Council.

Step 3 — the search and clip selection. The query is run against the stock library the user has configured (Pixabay or Pexels, both free, both honest about their licensing). The tool does not take the first result. It scores each result against the search terms and the scene's duration, prefers clips whose tags overlap with the query, and prefers portrait orientation for a Short. If the first query returns nothing usable, it falls back to the alternate, then to a two-word version, then to a generic city skyline. If all of that returns nothing, the scene becomes a card — but only as a last resort, and only because it is better than a mismatch.

Step 4 — the label. Every scene that has footage carries an Illustrative footage label in the corner of the video for the whole time the footage is on screen. It is small, unobtrusive, and unavoidable. It is the tool's way of saying, on every frame, that this is not footage of the event. The user can turn it off in the video settings, but the default is on, and the label is what makes a generic stock clip honest.

The whole pipeline — plan, search, select, label — runs on the user's own stock library API key. Nothing about it depends on a server. The scene plan is stored with the script, so the user can edit it before the video is recorded: change a search query, turn a footage scene into a card, turn a card back into footage. The point of the plan is not that the tool gets it right on the first try; the point is that the plan is visible and editable, so the tool does not have to.

The illustrative-footage label

The label exists for a specific reason, and it is worth stating plainly because most AI video tools do not do this.

A stock clip is not footage of the event. A clip of a missile launch is footage of some missile launch, somewhere, at some time. If the video plays it under a sentence about a specific strike, and there is nothing on screen saying otherwise, the viewer is entitled to assume the clip is the strike. That is not a viewer being careless; it is a viewer reading the video the way the video invites them to read it. The label is what removes the invitation. It says, on every frame the footage is on screen, that this is illustrative, not evidentiary. The viewer can then decide what to do with that information — most viewers, most of the time, will accept an illustrative clip and keep watching; but they will have been told.

The label matters most in three situations:

  • A story about a specific event. A specific strike, a specific disaster, a specific protest. Anything that happened at a specific place at a specific time. The label is what keeps a generic clip from being read as the specific one.
  • A story where the footage could imply a claim the script does not make. A clip of a missile launch under a sentence about a specific missile. A clip of a burning building under a sentence about a specific building that burned. A clip of a border crossing under a sentence about a specific border crossing.
  • A story where the platform's policy on inauthentic content applies. YouTube's inauthentic content policy targets videos that mislead viewers about what is being shown. An unlabelled stock clip is a low-stakes version of that problem. A labelled one is not.

There is one honest case for turning the label off: when the story is not about a specific event and the footage is purely illustrative by construction. A 45-second Short explaining how a central bank raises interest rates does not need a label on a clip of a bank building, because nobody thinks the clip is the central bank. A 45-second Short about the Fed's decision does, because the clip of a bank building next to "the Fed raised rates" reads as the Fed building even when it is not. The rule is not "label everything" or "label nothing"; the rule is "label when a viewer could misread the clip as footage of the event the script is about". That is the line, and most news videos are on the "label" side of it.

Good search: naval warship sea → returns a clip of a generic warship underway, chosen because it is a verb-noun shot of the class of thing the sentence is about, without claiming to be the warship. Paired with the same sentence, the video shows what the sentence is describing, without implying anything the sentence does not say. The illustrative label is on, and it is telling the truth: this is what the sentence is about, not the event itself.

The difference between the two searches is not the tool, and it is not the stock library. It is whether the search was written as a topic or as a shot. A topic search returns the closest thing to the topic, which for news is usually a dramatic generic clip that overclaims. A shot search returns a scene, which is almost always the right choice for a news video.

Frequently asked questions

What kind of stock footage should I use in a news Short?

Establishing shots and verb-noun shots, not action shots and not topic shots. An establishing shot — a wide view of a city, a building, a coastline — suggests a place without claiming a specific event. A verb-noun shot — missile launch, protest crowd, oil tanker — shows what the sentence is about without implying that it is the specific instance the sentence is describing. A topic shot — missile, sanctions, inflation — returns a generic, dramatic clip that overclaims.

Why is my stock footage misleading?

Because the clip is being read as footage of the event, when it is footage of a version of the event somewhere else. That is not a defect of the clip; it is a defect of the pairing. A clip of a missile launch under a sentence about a specific strike reads as the strike, even when the label is on. The fix is not to find a more dramatic clip — it is to find a less specific one, an establishing shot that suggests the place without claiming the event. If no such shot exists, the scene should be a card.

When should I use a text card instead of footage?

Whenever the sentence is abstract: an opinion, a claim, a statistic, a question to the viewer, the closing call-to-action. There is no stock clip that illustrates "whether this holds depends on two things" without implying a claim the sentence is not making. A plain caption card — big text on a dark background, no footage — keeps the visual track honest and stops the video from pretending to illustrate something that is not visual. A card is not a fallback; it is the right choice for the sentences that do not have a shot.

Do I need an illustrative-footage label?

Yes, in almost every case where a viewer could misread a stock clip as footage of the event the script is about. That covers most news videos, because most news videos are about specific events at specific places at specific times. The label is on by default in Autonom Newsreel's video studio, and the default is the right choice. Turning it off is honest when the story is not about a specific event — a general explainer about how interest rates work does not need a label on a bank building — but turning it off on a story about a specific strike, disaster, or protest is misleading the viewer, and the label is what makes the video honest.

Can I edit the footage plan before the video is recorded?

Yes, in the video step. Every scene in the script has a footage plan — footage or card, with a search query and an alternate — and the plan is shown as an editable list. You can change a search query, turn a footage scene into a card, or turn a card back into footage, and re-record the video without re-rendering the voice, because the voice is cached from the first run. The plan is the tool's best guess, not a fixed choice; the point of exposing it is so you can disagree with it, before the video is made.

Which stock library should I use?

Either. Pixabay and Pexels both have generous free tiers, both allow commercial use without attribution for most clips, and both have good enough coverage of the generic shots a news video needs. Pexels has a slight edge in portrait-orientation clips for Shorts; Pixabay has a slight edge in breadth of ordinary daily-life footage. The bigger decision is not which library — it is whether you configure a library at all. Without one, Autonom renders the video with a plain dark background and big captions, which is a perfectly usable style, and it never puts a clip on screen that is not labelled.
